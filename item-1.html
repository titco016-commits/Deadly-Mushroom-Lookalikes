Morels

True Morel Mushrooms

Morchella esculenta.

The genus Morchella, commonly known as true morels, consists of edible sac fungi recognized by their distinctive honeycomb-like caps formed by ridges and pits. Highly prized by chefs—especially in French and Catalan cuisine—morels are considered a delicacy but must be cooked properly, as they can be toxic when raw or undercooked. Because morels are notoriously difficult to cultivate, a major global industry has developed around harvesting them from the wild, particularly in North America, Turkey, China, the Himalayas, India, and Pakistan. Though long debated by mycologists, modern DNA research suggests there may be over seventy species of morels worldwide, many of which are unique to specific regions. The genus continues to be actively studied in areas such as classification, evolution, and geography, with new species still being identified.

True morels have a uniform honeycomb pattern with ridges and pits that run deep into the cap, and the cap is fully attached to the stem at the bottom, not hanging free. When sliced lengthwise from top to bottom, a true morel will be completely hollow inside, like a little mushroom tunnel. Their shape tends to be tall and elongated, and the texture is firm, not wavy or brain-like. These identifying traits are consistent across true morel species and are the safest way to confirm a genuine find.

False Morel Mushrooms

Gyromitra esculenta.

When foraging for morels, it’s crucial to carefully distinguish them from toxic lookalikes. Many false morels—and even true morels when raw—can cause illness, with some species remaining dangerous even after cooking. Certain false morels, including Gyromitra esculenta, contain toxic compounds like gyromitrin, which breaks down in the body into a harmful chemical linked to serious poisoning, long-term health risks, and even fatalities. Symptoms can range from stomach upset and loss of coordination to potentially life-threatening reactions. While some people consume false morels without obvious harm, poisonings most often occur when large quantities are eaten, when they are improperly prepared, or when they are consumed repeatedly over multiple days. Due to the seriousness of these risks, proper identification and caution are essential when gathering wild mushrooms.

False morels often appear wavy, wrinkled, or like a brain, rather than neatly honeycombed. Many have a cap that hangs freely from the stem or attaches only at the top, instead of being fully connected. When cut open, they are not hollow — instead they may be chambered, cottony, or filled with tissue. False morels also tend to be irregular in shape, lumpy, and sometimes reddish-brown. These differences help foragers spot imposters and avoid potentially toxic lookalikes.
